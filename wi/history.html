How this deployment grew

districtry Wisconsin launched on 25 August 2026 and has grown through four planned phases since. This page is its honest record: what the deployment ships, what it re-checks on a schedule, and the corrections its own machinery has caught along the way.

The changelog

  • 29 August 2026

    Monroe County's supervisors, and a refusal that measured the wrong thing

    The county board card now names the supervisor for each of Monroe County's sixteen districts, with the county e-mail address published for that seat. The county has published that list all along. This deployment had recorded Monroe's website as refusing automated readers, and had re-measured that refusal four separate times — from a browser user-agent, from this project's own, from no user-agent at all, and over plain http. Every one of those probes changed the same thing and left the rest of the request alone. The county's edge was not reading the user-agent: it was looking for the handful of headers a browser attaches to an ordinary page navigation, and a request carrying them is served the same page a person sees, from the same machine that had been refused. Re-probing the eight other counties filed under that refusal found four more that answer — Marathon, Outagamie, Rock and Sheboygan, 128 seats between them, now builds waiting on a reading rather than blocks. What ships here is read as a table rather than as lines, because the page carries a month of calendar dates above it and a line-reader would have been keying on a calendar: the columns are located by their own header names, and each row states its district twice — in the District cell and in the address the county maintains for that seat — with the two required to agree.

  • 27 August 2026

    Where you vote, everywhere in Wisconsin

    The ward card now names your polling place — its building, its street address, and a pin on the map at the building itself — for 7,131 of Wisconsin's 7,161 wards. Until today it could do that in two cities only. The state aggregates the pairing behind a lookup whose every route refuses automated readers, and this project had spent a fortnight measuring that refusal from every angle: each measurement was true, and each was recorded as though it described the world. It described a website. Asked directly, the Wisconsin Elections Commission sent the whole list in an afternoon. What arrived is the Commission's working copy for the 3 November election rather than its published edition, so every card says so in plain words, names the election it belongs to, dates the day it was read, keeps the state's own lookup as the way to confirm — and stops showing the pairing once that election is past, because a list of polling places that outlives its election looks current and is not. Milwaukee's and Madison's own published pairings still come first, since it is the city that runs the election. Twenty-nine wards in twelve municipalities are missing from the state's list, and their cards say so rather than guess. Building the join also turned up a fault nobody had noticed: twenty-six Wisconsin wards carry a letter in their number, and the card had been dropping it — so seven pairs of distinct Wauwatosa wards had been sharing one name on screen.

  • 27 August 2026

    The data-gaps panel learns where you are standing

    The panel that lists what this map cannot tell you now leads with the gaps that apply where you clicked, the way the Illinois deployment already did. It could not before, and the reason was invisible: every Wisconsin gap was recorded without the counties it applies to, and the county shapes needed to test a click against them were never built — so a reader standing on Milwaukee City Hall, having clicked, was told to click a spot on the map first. All seventy-two county shapes are now sliced from the county map this app already ships, so the two can never drift apart, and every gap names the counties it genuinely affects: the five with no fire-and-rescue filings, the fifty with no published board roster, the thirteen whose city ward filings are incomplete. Two statewide gaps are tagged to every county on purpose, because at county resolution it is better to mention a gap that does not quite reach your block than to tell you nothing is missing when something is. The misleading sentence was a fleet-wide fault and is fixed for every state at once.

  • 27 August 2026

    The police district card learns its captains' names

    Milwaukee's police-district card now names each district's commanding officer and phone number, read weekly from the city's own per-district pages — the first roster in this deployment whose source can only be reached from the project's scheduled infrastructure, never from a development machine, so the first copy was carried over from the weekly check's own verified run. All seven districts name their captain, and getting the seventh took reading the city's markup three ways: District 2's page splits the word Captain in half, the phone directory hides the district number in a way that once keyed every phone to District 2, and District 4 leaves its officer heading empty with the name in a link below it — each caught and pinned before any name shipped, so a page naming nobody would say so on the card rather than guess. Names never publish without review: every weekly change arrives as a proposed update a person reads first.

  • 27 August 2026

    Madison wards learn where they vote

    The ward card now names the polling place for each of Madison's 137 wards — the building, its address, and a map pin — the second city to gain the pairing after Milwaukee. Madison publishes it as one layer on the city's own open-data server: a polling point per ward, carrying the ward number, the building name, and the street address. The build shipped because the ward numbering agrees three ways — the polling layer, the city's own ward boundary layer, and the state ward file all name the same 137 wards exactly — and because every point lands inside Madison. One honesty note is stated on the card: the city's layer publishes no edition date, so the pairing is dated as the day it was read, and the state's MyVote lookup stays linked as the confirmation route. Outside these two cities the answer is unchanged: the statewide pairing sits behind surfaces that refuse automated readers, now an open ask to the state's election commission.

  • 27 August 2026

    Three careful visits to locked doors, and what each one proved

    A one-shot probe had shown the state election site's refusal was about the asking environment; a follow-up expedition went to see what the site actually holds, and came back with a harder truth: the front door opens, but every interior page that might carry data — the clerk directory, the statistics pages, the election results — challenges the visitor again and stays shut, seventeen attempts across two runs. So whether the state publishes the statewide polling-place pairing as data cannot be determined from here, and the question moves from software to people: it is now an ask to the commission itself. The same expedition delivered elsewhere — Milwaukee's police-district pages proved fully readable and name each district's commanding officer, so a weekly check of those names now runs, with the captains arriving on the police-district card once the first reviewed batch lands. Every visit obeyed the same rules: a genuine browser, bounded waits, and a refusal recorded as a refusal.

  • 27 August 2026

    The technical college district joins the map, honest about what nobody elects

    A new statewide layer answers which of Wisconsin's sixteen technical college districts your point funds through its property tax — and states plainly that the district's board is appointed under state law, not elected, so the card names no officeholder rather than inventing one. The geometry is the state education department's own, shipped only after five checks passed: exactly sixteen districts, each containing its own college's home city, none overlapping, and the map's one true hole being Lake Winnebago — which belongs to no district, so a reader clicking the lake correctly finds nothing. Two earlier drafts of that last check failed against reference maps that count the Great Lakes as part of the state, a reminder that even the checking has to be checked.

  • 27 August 2026

    Madison joins the city tier, and the state closes two districts the city's map still draws

    The tax-district layer now answers in Madison too — its second city — and a new layer names every neighborhood association the city registers at a point. Both came with lessons the map alone would have gotten wrong. Madison's own boundary layer mixes real tax districts with half-mile planning buffers in one file, and it still draws two districts the state's certified active list says are closed, while missing the newest district entirely — so what ships is only what the city's map, the city's program page and the state's list agree on, fourteen districts, with the undrawn fifteenth recorded as a gap rather than silently absent. The association layer taught the opposite lesson: registrations genuinely nest, condominium and resident associations inside neighborhood associations, so the card lists every association covering a point instead of the first one found, each labeled as what it is.

  • 27 August 2026

    The state election site's refusal proves to be about where you ask from

    For three weeks the record said Wisconsin's election commission website refuses automated readers — the reason the ward card links the state's polling-place lookup instead of naming your polling place. One deliberate test settled what kind of refusal that is: a probe run from this project's own scheduled infrastructure, driving a genuine browser and doing nothing to evade or defeat any control, found the site answers there — the challenge page clears in under four seconds, and the MyVote lookup answers plain requests outright. The block was about the asking environment, not the site. That does not put statewide polling places on the map by itself: no bulk data file has been located yet, and access can be withdrawn as easily as it was granted, so every follow-up keeps checking rather than assuming. But three questions this deployment had filed as blocked — statewide polling places, early-voting sites, a statewide municipal clerk list — are now questions about what the state publishes, not about whether this project can look. The same run found Milwaukee's city site equally readable from there, which queues a weekly check of each police district's commanding officer.

  • 26 August 2026

    Milwaukee wards learn where they vote

    The ward card now names the polling place for every one of Milwaukee's 356 wards — the building, its address, and the city's own entrance and parking guidance — from the city's open ward-to-polling-place dataset, with the pairing's date stated on the card and the state's MyVote lookup linked for confirmation. The build only shipped because three surfaces agree: the city's spreadsheet, the city's own map layer stating the same pairing per ward, and the state ward file whose numbers turn out to match the city's exactly, 1 through 356. Outside Milwaukee the honest answer is unchanged: the state's lookup refuses automated readers, so the card links it rather than guessing, and that gap stays on the record.

  • 26 August 2026

    The county card learns to check itself

    Phase 4 put every county officer on the county card — sheriff, district attorney, treasurer, clerk of circuit court, register of deeds, board chair, executive, and coroner or medical examiner, from the state's own Blue Book with elected-vs-appointed labels taken from the book's own codes — and then built the machinery that checks the book against the counties' own websites every week. The first runs earned it: Waukesha's page recorded that the book's county executive had died in office (the interim now ships from the county's page, and the builder refuses to ever regress to the stale name), Portage's pages named a different executive, seven more offices had turned over, and Sawyer's executive cell — which literally prints "Vacant" — had been shipping as a person's name. By the end of the day, 44 of 72 counties' officer rows carried weekly-verified contact and names; the other 28 counties' sites either refuse automated readers or publish nothing verifiable, each with its reason on record. The chair column also caught two state publications disagreeing about Menominee's board size (7 seats against 5 districts) — the card claims neither number.

  • 26 August 2026

    Milwaukee's city tier and the 911 map

    Phase 3 added ten layers. Statewide: 838 city and village council districts dissolved from the state's own ward filings, and the complete NG911 tier — which fire, law enforcement, and ambulance service covers a point, and which 911 center answers the call — from the state Office of Emergency Communications' aggregate of every county's own filing. Milwaukee got its own tier: the elected MPS school board, police districts and squad areas, the city's official neighborhoods, and its tax incremental districts. Counties whose 911 filings are absent or partial are re-measured on every build and recorded as gaps rather than papered over.

  • 26 August 2026

    Courts, wards, and who holds the county offices

    Phase 2 added seven layers: circuit courts and the court of appeals (both benches scraped weekly from the court system itself, under a double witness of statute and the courts' own directory), the state's live 7,161-ward layer, nearest police and fire stations, and statewide school and library locations. The county card gained every county's clerk — name, office, hours, phone, and e-mail — from the Blue Book cross-checked against the clerks' own association, and both legislative chambers gained the legislature's own office and contact data.

  • 25 August 2026

    Launch

    Wisconsin shipped as the fleet's fourth instance and its first to grow in place rather than as a separate fork: twelve statewide layers on day one, including all 1,590 county supervisory districts in a single fetch — possible because Wisconsin law (Wis. Stat. 5.15(4)(br)) makes every county file its boundaries with the legislature's technology bureau twice a year, and the bureau publishes the aggregate openly.
